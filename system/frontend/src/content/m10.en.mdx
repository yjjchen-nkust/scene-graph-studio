<Step id="s1">

## Three things a language model can be asked to do here

The literature since 2023 uses large models in three distinct roles, and conflating them is the
main source of confusion when reading it.

**As annotator.** The model does not produce the scene graph at test time. It repairs, extends or
relabels the training annotation, and a conventional model is then trained on the result. LLM4SGG
and GPT4SGG are here.

**As generator.** The model produces the scene graph directly at test time. IndVisSGG is here, and
so is every prompting result you have seen quoted.

**As reasoner.** The graph is not the output but the substrate: the model reasons over it, or is
trained by reinforcement with a graph-shaped reward. SaGe is here.

Ask which role a paper is in before reading its numbers. An annotator's numbers measure a trained
model; a generator's measure a prompt.

</Step>

<Step id="s2">

## Zero-shot collapse, and why it is not a vision failure

A frontier VLM asked plainly for VG-150 triplets scores R@50 of roughly 1 to 2. It describes the
scene correctly when asked in prose. The collapse is entirely in the vocabulary: M4's match
relation requires string identity on the predicate, and a model that answers `sitting on` where
the annotation says `on` has spent a ranked slot and left the truth unmatched.

LLM4SGG names this *semantic over-simplification* and is worth reading for the name alone. It is
the same phenomenon as M1's synonym merging, seen from the model's side rather than the dataset's.

Hold on to this when you reach M11, because the whole anchor paper is one answer to it.

</Step>

<Step id="s3">

## The annotator mode, where the gains are real

**LLM4SGG** uses a language model to extract triplets from image captions and align them to the
target vocabulary, producing supervision for images that had none.

**GPT4SGG** combines a holistic image description with region captions, so that the model reasons
about the whole scene and about the parts, then reconciles them.

**SVG and ROBIN** attack annotation incompleteness directly: given a graph, predict the relations
the annotator did not record. This is the most direct assault on the problem M3 named, and it is
the mode with the clearest measurable benefit, because the output is training data whose quality
can be checked by training on it.

**Verification over generation** — CAGE, ReLIC — is the mode's methodological conclusion. Asking a
model to check a candidate is a narrower question than asking it to produce one, and narrower
questions are answered more reliably. M11's audit stage is this idea applied inside a generator.

</Step>

<Step id="s4">

## The reasoner mode, and one honest caution

**Reinforcement learning with a graph-shaped reward** trains a model against a reward computed
from graph structure rather than from token likelihood. The appeal is obvious: the reward can
encode the thing you actually want.

**SaGe** treats the scene graph as a reasoning substrate — the graph is the intermediate
representation a downstream question is answered over, not the deliverable.

The caution applies to this whole module. Every result in it was produced with a proprietary model
at a particular version, and those versions are not reproducible. A number attached to "GPT-4V" is
a measurement of an artefact that no longer exists in that form. M11 says the same thing about its
own anchor paper, and it is the reason this course marks provenance on every figure rather than
only on the ones it doubts.

</Step>

<Step id="s5">

## In the lab

L7 runs the three modes over the same image with committed transcripts, so the comparison does not
depend on a live API or on a model version. Read the transcripts, not only the scores: the
annotator mode and the generator mode fail in visibly different ways.

</Step>

<Step id="s6">

## Checkpoint

A paper reports that its language-model pipeline reaches mR@50 of 21.4 on VG-150. Say which of the
three modes it must be in for that number to be a claim about a trained model rather than about a
prompt, and what in the paper would tell you.

</Step>
