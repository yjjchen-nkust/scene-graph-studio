<Step id="s1">

## Four years spent on the sign of one covariance

M4 ended with an identity: $R - mR = \operatorname{Cov}(n, R)/\bar n$. The gap between recall and
mean recall is the correlation between how often a predicate occurs and how well the model does on
it, and nothing else.

Read the debiasing literature with that identity in hand and its shape becomes clear. Every method
from TDE in 2020 to RA-SGG in 2024 is an attempt to drive that covariance toward zero, and every
one of them pays for it in $R$. The question is not whether they work but what they cost, and the
answer is in the arithmetic rather than in any paper's abstract.

</Step>

<Step id="s2">

<Intuition>

Reweighting the loss to favour rare predicates does exactly what it says: rare recall rises,
common recall falls. Since recall is dominated by the common classes, that second half is a direct
cost, and the trade-off is not a design flaw but arithmetic.

</Intuition>

<Formal>

$$
\max_{\theta}\ mR@k\quad\text{s.t.}\quad R@k\ge R_0;\qquad \text{FlowSG: both rise, the constraint stops binding}
$$

</Formal>

<Worked>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{debiasing reweights the loss: } \ \ell_\theta &= \textstyle\sum_p \omega_p\,\ell_p, \qquad \omega_p\propto n_p^{-\beta}\\
\beta\uparrow \;&\Rightarrow\; R_p\uparrow \text{ for rare } p, \quad R_p\downarrow \text{ for common } p\\
&\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{Cov}(n,R)\downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; R-mR \downarrow \quad\text{(Proposition 3)}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Worked>

<Implications>

$$
\begin{gathered}\text{Since } R=\textstyle\sum_p \frac{n_p}{N}R_p \text{ is dominated by large } n_p, \text{ lowering common-class recall}
\\ \text{costs } R \text{ directly. That is the trade-off, and why it held from TDE (2020) to RA-SGG (2024).}
\\[4pt]
\text{FlowSG (CVPR 2026) reports } 36.5\ R@50 \text{ and } 18.4\ mR@50 \text{ together: the constraint stopped binding.}\end{gathered}
$$

So the eight methods named in this module's title are eight points on one curve, and comparing
them on $mR$ alone is meaningless: any of them can be pushed further up the $mR$ axis by raising
$\beta$, at a cost in $R$ that the paper may or may not report.

The four families are worth separating, because they intervene at four different places.
**TDE** subtracts a counterfactual at inference, leaving training untouched. **CogTree and DLFE**
work on the loss. **NICE** says the labels themselves are wrong and relabels. **IETrans and ST-SGG**
change the data, transferring and self-training on the tail. **PE-Net** learns prototypes;
**RA-SGG** treats the annotation as partial rather than complete.

The last two are the interesting ones, because they stop treating the annotation as ground truth —
which M3 told you it is not.

</Implications>

</Step>

<Step id="s3">

## What would count as solving it

A method escapes the trade-off if it raises $mR$ without lowering $R$ — that is, if it raises
per-predicate recall on the tail without spending head recall. Nothing in the 2020–2024 sequence
does that, which is why the constraint in the optimisation above binds throughout.

This module's single claim records a 2026 report of both rising together, marked `verified: false`:
this course has not opened that table. Treat it as a claim to check rather than a result to teach,
and note what checking it would require — the same split, the same constraint mode, the same $k$,
and the same predicate merging as whatever it is being compared against.

</Step>

<Step id="s4">

## In the lab

L3 puts $\alpha$ on a slider, moving continuously from $R$ at $\alpha = 1$ to $mR$ at
$\alpha = 0$, over the same fixed predictions. Watch the ranking of the models change as you move
it. That reordering is the entire debiasing literature in one control.

</Step>

## Checkpoint

A method reports mR@50 four points above the previous best and does not report R@50. State what
you can and cannot conclude, and give the one additional number that would settle it.

</Step>
