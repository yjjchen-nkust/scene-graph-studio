<Step id="s1">

## What the graph is for

Fourteen modules have treated the scene graph as the thing to be produced and scored. This one
asks what it is produced *for*, and the answer reorganises the whole course.

Four uses, and they make different demands.

**Conditioning image generation.** A graph specifies a layout and a set of relations, and a
generator renders it. The hard part is not generating — it is measuring whether the rendered image
actually satisfies the relations it was given, which turns out to be the same matching problem as
M3 with neither side annotated.

**Probing what vision-language models understand.** ARO, SugarCrepe and VSR test whether a model
distinguishes `cup on table` from `table on cup`. Many do not, and the failure is exactly M0's
asymmetry: a model trained on bag-of-words alignment has no reason to encode direction.

**Memory and retrieval.** A scene graph is a queryable index over a scene or a video, which is
what makes it useful as an agent's memory and as the retrieval structure in a RAG pipeline.

**Fusion with knowledge graphs.** The scene graph says what is in front of the camera; a knowledge
graph says what is true in general. Joining them is old in symbolic AI and newly practical.

</Step>

<Step id="s2">

## Relation hallucination, and the neighbouring field

**Hallucinated relations** are the failure mode this course is best equipped to name. A VLM asked
to describe a scene will report plausible relations that are not present, and the reason is the
same frequency prior that made FREQ a strong baseline in M4: `person riding horse` is common, so a
person near a horse becomes a person riding one.

**HOI detection** is the neighbouring field, and it is worth knowing that it is neighbouring rather
than identical. Human-object interaction detection restricts the subject to a person and the
predicate to an action, which makes the problem smaller and the metrics different. Results do not
transfer, and neither do the baselines.

</Step>

<Step id="s3">

## Where the field stopped using its own metrics

Two application areas are worth naming because they made the same decision independently.

**Driving scene graphs** report task metrics — collision rate, planning success, time to
intervention — and not R@K. The graph is an intermediate representation and its quality is
measured by what depends on it.

**Surgical scene graphs** report per-relation F1, which has a precision term. They can afford one
because their annotation is exhaustive: a surgical video is annotated by clinicians against a
protocol, so an unrecorded relation is genuinely absent rather than merely unrecorded.

Read those two facts together and M3's rule — that a missing annotation counts as a mistake —
stands revealed as a consequence of Visual Genome's annotation process rather than a property of
the problem. Change the annotation and the metric changes with it.

</Step>

<Step id="s4">

## The datasets nobody teaches, and why they matter

**IndoorVG** merges Visual Genome classes into a smaller indoor vocabulary. M1 gave the arithmetic
of what merging does to mean recall; this is that arithmetic applied to a whole dataset, and it is
why `indoorvg` figures cannot be placed beside `vg150-sgb` figures.

**Haystack** ships explicit negatives — relations annotated as *not* holding. That is the only
dataset in this course that makes precision computable, and the reason is worth stating plainly:
precision needs to know what is false, and no other dataset records it.

**GQA, VrR-VG, SpatialSense and UnRel** each re-annotate or re-filter Visual Genome for a
different purpose, and each produces numbers that look comparable with VG150's and are not.

**The industrial gap** is what produced ISG, and M11 covered it. This project's **mini-ISG** is
built by the same method from openly licensed frames and is labelled as this course's teaching set,
never as the authors'. It is small, it is honest about being small, and it exists so that the last
lab runs on something.

</Step>

<Step id="s5">

## The two gaps

**The measurement gap.** The field's metric cannot distinguish a wrong prediction from a correct
one the annotator did not write down, and every consequence in this course follows from that: no
precision, a frequency baseline that beats learned models, a debiasing literature trading one
number against another, and a mask-pairing correction ignored for two years. Haystack shows the gap
is closable. Nothing else in the field has closed it.

**The deployment gap.** Almost every result in this course was produced on photographs of everyday
scenes, and almost every stated application is industrial, surgical, or robotic. The vocabularies
do not overlap, the annotation budgets do not resemble each other, and the two areas that did
deploy — driving and surgery — stopped using the field's metrics to do it.

A student who finishes this course should be able to say which of the two gaps a new paper is
addressing, and to notice when it claims to address one while measuring the other.

</Step>

<Step id="s6">

## In the lab

L8 is the course's last, and it is open: take a graph you built in L1, and use it. Condition a
layout on it, query it as memory, or test whether a model can tell it from its reverse.

Then write down which metric you used, and why that one.

</Step>

<Step id="s7">

## Checkpoint

Surgical scene graph work reports per-relation F1 and Visual Genome work cannot. Explain the
difference in one sentence, then say what would have to change about Visual Genome for the two to
report the same metric.

</Step>
