<Step id="s1">

## What a label cannot say

A classifier looking at a kitchen returns `person`, `cup`, `table`. So does a classifier looking
at a photograph of a person holding a cup over a table, and at one of a cup already on the table
with the person walking away. The three labels are identical; the scenes are not.

The missing thing is not a better label. It is the relation between the labelled things. A scene
graph is the smallest representation that carries it: the objects, and the directed, named edges
between them.

</Step>

<Step id="s2">

## Three layers of one image

One image, with labels, boxes and relations as three independent layers. What remains when every layer is off is what a classifier sees; bringing them up one at a time is what a scene graph adds.

<Step id="s3">

## Annotation density and the candidate space

The density slider moves the annotated edge count and leaves the candidate count where it is. The ratio between them is why every metric in this unit is stated as a recall.

<Playground kp="F1" part="2" />

</Step>

<Step id="s4">

<Intuition>

A scene graph names what is in the image and, separately, how those things stand to one another.
The second half is what a list of labels throws away. It is also where nearly all the difficulty
is, because the number of possible relations grows with the square of the number of objects while
the annotation budget does not.

</Intuition>

<Formal>

A scene graph over an image is a directed, labelled multigraph

$$
G = (V, E), \qquad E \subseteq V \times \mathcal{P} \times V
$$

where $V$ is the set of localized objects, each an image region with a class, and $\mathcal{P}$
is a fixed set of predicate classes. An element of $E$ is written as a triplet

$$
\langle s, p, o \rangle, \qquad s, o \in V,\; p \in \mathcal{P}
$$

read *subject, predicate, object*. The triplet is ordered: $\langle s, p, o \rangle$ and
$\langle o, p, s \rangle$ are different claims about the image.

</Formal>

<Worked>

Take the kitchen above, with $V = \{v_1{:}\,\texttt{person},\, v_2{:}\,\texttt{cup},\,
v_3{:}\,\texttt{table}\}$ and $\mathcal{P} \supseteq \{\texttt{holding}, \texttt{on},
\texttt{above}\}$.

The first photograph is

$$
E_1 = \{\langle v_1, \texttt{holding}, v_2 \rangle,\; \langle v_2, \texttt{above}, v_3 \rangle\}
$$

and the second is

$$
E_2 = \{\langle v_2, \texttt{on}, v_3 \rangle\}
$$

Same $V$, different $E$, and the difference is the whole content of the scene. Count the
possibilities: with $|V| = 3$ there are $|V|(|V|-1) = 6$ ordered pairs, and with
$|\mathcal{P}| = 50$ predicates there are $300$ candidate triplets over three objects alone. At
$|V| = 20$, which is an ordinary photograph, there are $380$ pairs and $19{,}000$ candidates.

</Worked>

<Implications>

Three things follow, and each one returns later in the course.

**The edge set is sparse and the candidate set is not.** A model must rank $O(|V|^2|\mathcal{P}|)$
possibilities against a ground truth holding a few dozen edges. This is why every metric in this
field is a recall at a rank cutoff, and never an accuracy.

**Direction carries meaning that symmetry destroys.** $\langle \texttt{cup}, \texttt{on},
\texttt{table} \rangle$ is true and $\langle \texttt{table}, \texttt{on}, \texttt{cup} \rangle$ is
false, of the same photograph. A representation that stores an unordered pair cannot express the
difference, and a model scored on unordered pairs is not being asked the question.

**What is annotated is not what is true.** Visual Genome's edges are what somebody wrote down,
not everything that holds in the scene, so an edge the model proposes and the annotation lacks may
be a mistake or may be correct and unrecorded. The metric cannot tell the two apart, and the
course returns to this as the difference between *missed* and *spurious* in the diff.

</Implications>

</Step>

<Step id="s5">

## The candidate space, and direction

Six objects. Build a few edges and read them against the total number of candidate triplets.

Turning off directed arrows halves the candidate space and merges two edges that were distinct into one. The panel lists the edges that can no longer be told apart.

<Step id="s6">

## Swapping subject and object

Take a triplet, swap its subject and object, and see whether it is still recorded in E.

Note what the reading is: *not recorded in E* and *false* are different claims. The first is a fact about the annotation, the second about the scene, and this course keeps them apart throughout.

<Playground kp="F8" />

</Step>

<Step id="s7">

## What you will do in the lab

L1 hands you an image with its objects already boxed and asks you to build $E$ yourself: click a
subject, click an object, name the predicate. Your graph is then scored against the annotation
under PredCls, which gives you the boxes and the labels so that the only thing measured is the
structure you proposed.

</Step>

<Step id="s8">

## Checkpoint

Write down, for the photograph in front of you, one triplet whose reverse is also true and one whose reverse is false. If you cannot find the first, say why the predicate set makes it hard.

</Step>
