import { Formal, Implications, Intuition, Worked } from './math/Parts';

<Step id="s1">

## Stop enumerating pairs

M5 ended on a ratio: at VG150's 50 predicates, about one decision in a thousand is positive for an
image of twelve objects, and about one in forty-five thousand once a detector keeps 80 boxes.
One-stage methods take that seriously and predict the set of triplets directly, with no pair
enumeration anywhere. The mechanism they borrow is DETR's: a fixed number of queries, each of which
may emit one prediction, matched to the ground truth by a bipartite assignment computed per image.

The change is not a speed optimisation. It replaces "which of these pairs is related" with "what
is in this image", and the two questions have different failure modes.

</Step>

<Step id="s2">

<Intuition>

The model emits an unordered set, and the loss needs to know which prediction is being judged
against which annotation. The assignment that minimises total cost is computed first, then the
loss is applied through it. Everything downstream depends on $q$, the number of queries, and $q$
is chosen before training.

</Intuition>

<Formal>

$$
\hat\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{\lvert G\rvert}\left[w_c\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}(i,\sigma(i))+w_b\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}}(i,\sigma(i))\right]
$$

</Formal>

<Worked>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\sigma &= \arg\min_{\sigma}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\lvert G\rvert}\mathcal{L}\bigl(i,\sigma(i)\bigr),
\qquad \mathcal{L}=w_c\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}+w_b\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}}\\
\text{cost} &= O(q^3) \ \text{by the Hungarian algorithm, } q=\text{number of queries}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Worked>

<Implications>

$$
\begin{gathered}q<\lvert G\rvert \;\Rightarrow\; \lvert G\rvert-q \text{ ground-truth triplets are unreachable: recall is capped at } q/\lvert G\rvert.
\\ q\gg\lvert G\rvert \;\Rightarrow\; q-\lvert G\rvert \text{ slots train only against } \varnothing \text{ and stay undertrained.}
\\[4pt]
\text{SpeaQ answers with groupwise specialization; Hydra-SGG with one-to-many assignment.}\end{gathered}
$$

Read the two bounds as a design constraint that no amount of training removes. Too few queries and
recall has a ceiling the model cannot reach whatever it learns. Too many and most queries see only
the no-object class during training, so they never become useful.

That is why the later entries in this module's title exist. SpeaQ specialises queries into groups
so that a query is responsible for a kind of relation rather than for a slot. Hydra-SGG assigns one
prediction to several ground-truth triplets, which softens the "one query, one answer" rule that
creates the undertrained tail.

Note also what $q$ does to the speed-accuracy frontier: the Hungarian step is cubic in $q$, so the
two failure modes cannot both be escaped by making $q$ large.

</Implications>

</Step>

<Step id="s3">

## Seven designs, and what each one moved

**FCSGG** predicts relation affinity fields — a dense, per-pixel vector field — rather than
discrete pairs. It is the fastest thing in the literature and the least accurate, which makes it
the left-hand end of the frontier.

**RelTR** couples subject and object queries so that one query carries both ends of a triplet.

**SGTR** builds a bipartite graph between entity and predicate proposals, so the pairing is
learned rather than enumerated or assumed.

**EGTR** extracts relations from the detector's own decoder attention, on the observation that a
detector already computes object-to-object interaction and then discards it.

**DSGG** treats the whole thing as one graph-prediction problem; **SpeaQ** specialises queries by
group; **Hydra-SGG** uses hybrid one-to-many assignment; **REACT** is the recent efficiency entry.

The honest summary is that the frontier moved a long way in speed and much less in accuracy. When
you read a one-stage table, read the latency column first.

</Step>
